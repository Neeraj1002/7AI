[DOCUMENTED] About 1 pm on 4 December 1872, by land time (5 December by sea time), the Dei Gratia was in the open Atlantic, midway between the Azores and mainland Portugal. 2. [DOCUMENTED] The Dei Gratia's helmsman reported a vessel heading unsteadily toward her, about six miles off; the ship's erratic movement and the odd set of her sails made Captain David Morehouse suspect something was wrong. 3. [DOCUMENTED] No one was seen on deck and signals got no reply, so Morehouse sent first mate Oliver Deveau and second mate John Wright across by boat; they read the name Mary Celeste on her stern, climbed aboard and found the ship deserted. 4. [DOCUMENTED] The ship was built at Spencer's Island, Nova Scotia, and launched on 18 May 1861 as the Amazon; she was sold to American owners and renamed Mary Celeste in 1868; a $10,000 refit in early 1872 enlarged her; Briggs held four of twelve shares. 5. [DOCUMENTED] Captain Benjamin Briggs, born in 1835 in Wareham, Massachusetts, sailed with his wife Sarah and their two-year-old daughter Sophia; their son Arthur stayed at home with his grandmother. 6. [DOCUMENTED] There were seven crew: first mate Albert Richardson, second mate Andrew Gilling, steward Edward Head, and four German seamen, the brothers Volkert and Boz Lorenzen, Arian Martens and Gottlieb Goudschaal. Ten people were aboard. 7. [DOCUMENTED] The cargo was 1,701 barrels of alcohol, described as industrial alcohol, bound from New York for Genoa. 9. [DOCUMENTED] She left her pier on 5 November, waited off Staten Island for better weather and entered the Atlantic on 7 November. The Dei Gratia, a brigantine from Nova Scotia loading petroleum at Hoboken for Genoa via Gibraltar, left New York on 15 November, eight days after her. 10. [DOCUMENTED] The sails were partly set and in poor condition, some missing; the main hatch cover was secure, but the fore and lazarette hatches were open with their covers beside them; the only lifeboat, a small yawl, was missing; the compass binnacle had shifted and its glass cover was broken. 11. [DOCUMENTED] About three and a half feet of water was in the hold, significant but not alarming for a ship of her size; a makeshift sounding rod lay on deck; one of her two pumps was out of order or disassembled. 12. [DOCUMENTED] The cabins were wet and untidy from water that came in through doorways and skylights; the galley stove had been knocked out of place; there was no meal on the cabin table and nothing cooking. 13. [DOCUMENTED] The belongings of the captain and crew were still aboard, including a sheathed sword under the captain's bed and a woman's and a child's clothing and toys; about six months of food and water remained. 14. [DOCUMENTED] The captain's chronometer, sextant and navigation book and the ship's register were missing, along with most of the ship's papers; the log lay in the mate's cabin and the log slate on the cabin table. 15. [DOCUMENTED] The last entry on the log slate, made on the morning of 25 November, put her six miles from Santa Maria in the Azores, nearly 400 nautical miles from where the Dei Gratia found her. 16. [DOCUMENTED] At Gibraltar, surveyors found the barrels well stowed except one that had been started; at Genoa, nine of the 1,701 barrels were found empty, through seepage or minor damage. 17. [DOCUMENTED] Morehouse sent Deveau and two seamen aboard and brought her to Gibraltar, 600 nautical miles away; a salvor could expect a substantial share of the value of ship and cargo. 18. [DOCUMENTED] The popular version describes every sail set, the galley fire burning and a meal untasted on the table. The Los Angeles Times printed such details in June 1883, and another often-repeated version added a half-eaten breakfast and lukewarm tea. 19. [DOCUMENTED] The Dei Gratia's master reported only the jib and the fore staysail set, not every sail, and the court testimony gives no evidence of a cat, a detail some retellings add. 20. [DOCUMENTED] In January 1884 Arthur Conan Doyle, writing anonymously in the Cornhill Magazine, published a short story that renamed the ship Marie Celeste and invented a fictional mutiny; the spelling Marie became more common than Mary. 21. [DOCUMENTED] A 1913 Strand Magazine "survivor" account, and a 1920s "survivor" hoax by the Irish writer Laurence Keating that the New York Herald Tribune believed in 1926, both contained basic errors. 22. [DOCUMENTED] Her first captain, Robert McLellan, died on her maiden voyage in 1861, and Captain Edgar Tuthill died at St Helena in 1879, which encouraged talk that she was cursed. 23. [DOCUMENTED] In January 1885 Captain Gilman Parker deliberately ran her onto the Rochelois reef off Haiti as part of an insurance fraud. At the Boston trial in July 1885 the jury could not agree on the conspiracy charge; the claims were withdrawn and repaid, and Parker was allowed to go free; he died in poverty three months later. 24. [DOCUMENTED] In August 2001 an expedition led by Clive Cussler announced that it had found her wreck on the Rochelois reef; tests later showed that the timbers came from trees still growing in 1894, about ten years after she was lost. 25. [DOCUMENTED] The hearings opened in Gibraltar on 17 December 1872 under Chief Justice Sir James Cochrane; Attorney General Frederick Solly-Flood, who was also Proctor for the Queen in Admiralty, ran the inquiry. 26. [DOCUMENTED] Solly-Flood came to believe that a crime had been committed: that the crew got at the alcohol, killed the Briggs family and officers, cut the bows to fake a collision, and fled in the yawl. He also suspected the Dei Gratia's crew of hiding something. 27. [DOCUMENTED] The surveyor John Austin reported cuts on both sides of the bow, which he thought were made by a sharp instrument, and possible traces of blood on the captain's sword; Royal Navy captains later reported stains on a rail that might be blood. 28. [DOCUMENTED] A Gibraltar chemist, Dr J. Patron, found that the stains were not blood; US Navy Captain Shufeldt judged that the marks on the bow came from the sea working the ship's timbers. 29. [DOCUMENTED] The court found no evidence of foul play. Flood released the ship on 25 February 1873, and she sailed for Genoa under Captain George Blatchford. 30. [DEBATED] The court awarded the Dei Gratia's master and crew £1,700. The Gibraltar Chronicle put that at about one-fifth of the court's valuation of ship and cargo; the Smithsonian puts it at one-sixth of the insured value; one authority thought it should have been two or three times higher. 31. [DOCUMENTED] Flood could not accept that an unmanned ship could have travelled so far. The historian Charles Fay cites a ship, the William L. White, that drifted unmanned for ten months in 1888 and 1889. A 2007 documentary investigation, using historical weather data, concluded that Mary Celeste could have sailed from her 25 November position to where she was found without a crew. 32. [DEBATED] The court's own evidence disagreed: Deveau described wet cabins on 4 December, while the court's surveyor reported dry chests and clothing on 23 December. 33. [DEBATED] Briggs's cousin Oliver Cobb and others favoured the idea that alcohol fumes, rumbling from the hold or a small explosion frightened Briggs into abandoning ship; critics point out that no sign of fire or blast damage was found. 34. [DEBATED] In 2006 the chemist Andrea Sella of University College London set off a butane explosion in a model of the hold for Channel 5; it made a pressure wave with no soot and no scorching. In 2026 two University of Manchester chemists used wood and ethanol in a model ship and concluded that a rapid ethanol-vapour explosion could leave no trace. 35. [DEBATED] Deveau thought Briggs may have left after a false sounding, because the pumps malfunctioned. A 2007 documentary investigation proposed a pump fouled by coal dust and refit debris, a chronometer error that put the ship about 120 miles west of where Briggs thought, and heavy weather the night before; it offered a scenario but no definite conclusion. 37. [DEBATED] No convincing evidence supported murder, piracy or a salvage plot between Briggs and Morehouse. The claim that the two captains dined together the night before she sailed rests on the recollection of Morehouse's widow fifty years later. 38. [UNKNOWN] None of the ten people aboard was ever seen or heard from again. 39. [UNKNOWN] It is not known where and when she was abandoned, or what the original log said beyond the transcribed last days; the log is believed to have been lost in 1885. It is also unknown whether a line tied the yawl to her.
