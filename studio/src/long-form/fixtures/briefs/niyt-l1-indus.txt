Facts: 1. Harappa, one of the largest Indus cities, dates to about 2600 to 1900 BCE, from calibrated radiocarbon dates. 2. More than 1,000 Harappan sites are known. 3. The largest cities may have held roughly 40,000 to 80,000 people. 4. Streets were laid out in a grid. 5. Fired-brick houses followed a standard brick proportion of 1:2:4. 6. Houses were reportedly connected to underground drains. 7. A house had a kitchen opening from the courtyard, with a hearth or brick-built fireplace. 8. Wheat and barley were the main staples, presumably made into bread and perhaps also cooked with water as gruel or porridge. 9. Lentils, peas and chickpeas were eaten, and sesame was grown for oil. 10. Dates, jujube, grapes and figs were eaten. 11. Meat came mainly from cattle; chickens, buffaloes and some sheep and goats were also kept. 12. Many fish were dried or salted, and marine fish bones were found at Harappa, far inland. 13. Cubical stone weights follow a binary series of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 times the smallest weight. 14. The smallest weight is 0.856 grams and the most common is about 13.7 grams. 15. Larger weights switch to decimal steps; the largest is 100 times the common weight. 16. The weights were only roughly alike from place to place, and each size shows significant variability. 17. Many small weights were likely used for light goods, under about half a kilogram, such as precious materials and pigments. 18. One scholar states there was no money economy and trade worked through barter or exchange of goods. 19. The weights may have been used for controlling trade and possibly for collecting taxes. 20. Shell working, ceramics, and agate and glazed steatite bead making were practised at Harappa from its earliest levels to its final phase. 21. Beads were drilled with copper drills and with tapered chert drills. 22. Harappan seals have been found at Ur and Lagash in Mesopotamia. 23. Clay tags attached to traded goods have turned up in the Mesopotamia region. 24. Slightly over 400 basic signs are known, though some researchers argue they reduce to about 39 elementary signs. 25. Thousands of inscriptions are known from some 60 excavation sites. 26. The average inscription is about five signs long, and none is longer than 26 signs, according to one count. 27. The script is undeciphered and the language it represents is unknown. 28. The Indus civilization emerged about 5,200 years ago and slowly disintegrated between 3,900 and 3,000 years ago. 29. Weaker monsoon rains reduced river flows; the researchers say this "suggests" a critical role in the civilization's rise and fall. 30. By about 3,900 years ago people shifted east toward the Ganges basin, where monsoon rains stayed reliable. 31. Settlements became smaller and more local, and cities declined; agriculture continued, while urban arts such as writing faded. 32. In 1947 the archaeologist Mortimer Wheeler suggested skeletons found at Mohenjo-daro were evidence of an "Aryan invasion". 33. In 1964 George Dales argued they were "not massacre victims at all"; a later study suggests they likely represent squatters from the west, and its author calls this tentative. 34. The site shows no evidence of invasion, and the scattered skeletons in later levels do not indicate warfare or raiding. 35. One archaeologist says there is no good evidence for rulers in the Indus civilization. 36. The causes of the decline are still debated.
