What happened to the ten days that vanished from the calendar in October 1582?

For centuries Europe used the Julian calendar, which added a leap day every four years. It was slightly too long, and by the sixteenth century the calendar had drifted about ten days away from the seasons. Easter was slipping later into the spring.

How do you fix ten days of drift? In February 1582 Pope Gregory XIII ordered a reform. The leap-year rule was changed so that century years are leap years only when divisible by four hundred. And to bring the calendar back in step, the days from the fifth to the fourteenth of October 1582 were simply skipped. In the countries that adopted the change at once, Thursday the fourth of October was followed by Friday the fifteenth.

Did everyone follow? No. Catholic countries such as Spain, Portugal and parts of Italy changed immediately. Britain and its colonies held out until 1752, when the gap had grown to eleven days. In September of that year, the second was followed by the fourteenth.

So the days did not disappear from time itself. They disappeared from the count. The reform is the reason our calendar stays in step with the seasons today.
