What almost happened to the most famous tower in the world? It was nearly torn down and sold for scrap, just twenty years after it opened. But before that, why was it built at all? The city needed a centerpiece for the 1889 World's Fair, marking one hundred years since the French Revolution. Designed at Gustave Eiffel's engineering company, the first sketch actually came from two of his engineers, Maurice Koechlin and Emile Nouguier, before Eiffel took the project forward.

How do you even build something that tall? Construction took two years, two months and five days, running from 1887 to 1889, and the tower opened on 31 March 1889. It was assembled from 18,038 pieces of wrought iron, held together by two and a half million rivets. At 300 metres, it became the tallest man-made structure on Earth, a title it would hold until the Chrysler Building overtook it in 1930.

So why was this record-breaking tower almost destroyed? Its construction permit was only for 20 years. Once that permit expired, the city of Paris had the right to dismantle it completely and clear the site. For a structure built purely as a temporary fairground centerpiece, that looked like the end.

So how did it survive? Eiffel turned his tower into a scientific tool the city couldn't afford to lose. He used it for meteorological and aerodynamic experiments, and later as a radio antenna, giving it a practical purpose beyond spectacle. That usefulness convinced the city to keep it standing instead of tearing it down. Next time you see it, remember it wasn't saved by beauty alone, but by the research still happening inside it.
